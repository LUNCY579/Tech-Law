# Chapter 1 — Incorporation, governance and fiduciary oversight

## 1.1 What incorporation legally creates
A corporation is a separate legal entity, not just a website with a brand. Delaware corporate existence commences under **8 Del. C. §106** in connection with the required certificate filing; compare §§101–103 for incorporation mechanics. A federal **C corporation** is a tax classification, whereas a **Delaware corporation** is an entity created under Delaware law. Do not equate the labels.

**Entity-choice framework:** Compare (a) investor expectations and financing instruments; (b) ownership flexibility; (c) governance costs; (d) tax classification and potential pass-through treatment; (e) state nexus, filings and taxes; (f) personal liability exceptions and corporate formalities. Neither a C corporation nor an LLC categorically minimizes all startup costs.

**Source:** [Delaware Code, Chapter 1, Subchapter I](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc01/index.html).

## 1.2 What belongs in the certificate
Under **§102(a)** the certificate addresses fundamentals including name, registered agent, purpose, authorized stock information and incorporator. It can include permitted optional provisions. Under **§102(b)(7)** a qualifying certificate can contain certain limitations on liability, subject to statutory exceptions. An exculpatory clause is **not** unlimited immunity, nor does every startup automatically have one.

### Close reading method
For any required certificate provision, identify the statutory minimum, the company's proposed language, what the board/shareholders must approve later, and what third parties might rely on. Use the operative enacted section rather than an unreviewed website template.

## 1.3 Board authority is distinct from founder ownership
**§141(a)** generally assigns management of the corporation's business and affairs to or under a board of directors, subject to statutory/certificate qualifications. Holding most shares is not identical to personally having unrestricted power to issue stock or approve transactions. Common steps: incorporator action, initial directors, organizational consents, bylaws, officers, share issuance approvals, stock ledger and documented consideration.

**Source:** [§141 official text](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc004/index.html).

## 1.4 Fiduciary duties and process
Directors' duties of care and loyalty are developed in Delaware corporate case law; statutory mechanisms such as §102(b)(7) interact with particular claims. *Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985), is an important historical process case, **but the official full text and subsequent-treatment check remain pending** in this repository. Do not quote or generalize that case as an authenticated holding from an unverified source.

**Founder hypothetical:** A three-director board approves issuing 20% of the company to one director's sibling without conflicts review or documented rationale. Identify (1) who has authority; (2) conflicts and independence; (3) financial fairness; (4) board and shareholder approvals; (5) proper records; (6) when separate legal counsel is necessary.

**Discussion:** A valid corporate act is not simply one that the CEO approves by email. The economic, governance and conflict record matters. Avoid deciding the legality solely from the percentage.

## 1.5 Completion checklist
- Certified filed certificate and registered agent.
- Accurate bylaws / director and officer appointments.
- Board and shareholder consents as applicable.
- Authorized-share and issued-share ledger reconciliation.
- Conflict-of-interest review procedures.
- Banking, tax, franchise and foreign-qualification checklist (jurisdiction-specific).
- IP assignment documentation and an approval record.

### Examination
**Question:** A founder says “I own 60%, so I can issue myself another 5 million shares.” Name five missing legal facts.

**Model answer:** Authorized-but-unissued shares; board authority and required approvals; existing agreements and preemptive/contractual rights; consideration and fairness/conflicts; any class-specific charter rights. Shareholder votes and fiduciary duties may also matter. The exact outcome depends on charter/bylaws and governing law.
