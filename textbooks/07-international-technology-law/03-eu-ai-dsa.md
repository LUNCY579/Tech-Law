# Chapter 3 — EU AI Act, Digital Services Act and platform duties

## 1. Two distinct regulatory regimes
The EU **AI Act**, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, regulates specified AI actors and uses. The **Digital Services Act**, Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, concerns certain online intermediary services, including hosting and platforms. Neither automatically replaces the GDPR, labor law, consumer law or contractual duties.

AI Act official [Article 113 consolidated explanation](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-113?full=1), [implementation timeline](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/eu-ai-act-implementation-timeline); DSA [Commission Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/digital-services-act-questions-and-answers).

## 2. AI Act role-by-role analysis
1. Identify whether the service is an **AI system** or involves a general-purpose AI model under Article 3.
2. Determine Article 2 territorial scope and role: provider, deployer, importer, distributor, product manufacturer or GPAI provider as relevant.
3. Evaluate Article 5 prohibited uses; Article 6 and Annex III high-risk conditions; Article 50 transparency duties; GPAI provisions where relevant.
4. Verify **operative version and dates**. Under the official 27 July 2026 consolidation, the Act phases in; certain Annex III high-risk obligations are scheduled for **2 December 2027**, with Annex I product-related high-risk obligations **2 August 2028**. Relevant 2025/2026 rules already apply. Read amendments and transitional conditions before implementation.
5. Link legal duties to technical evidence, control owners, instructions, monitoring, and independent validation claims.

**Employment example:** Candidate ranking used to shortlist applicants may implicate Annex III; a tool solely correcting spelling in a job description is not automatically classified identically. Intended purpose, actual use and Article 6 criteria matter.

## 3. DSA actor classification
The Commission's official guidance distinguishes **mere conduit**, **caching**, **hosting**, **online platforms**, and very large platforms/search engines. Duties depend on service and category. Some smaller businesses have exemptions from specific obligations; don't apply obligations meant for designated very large platforms to every document SaaS.

**Example:** A private document analysis app and a public marketplace hosting third-party listings have different DSA fact patterns. If users publicly share third-party content through a workspace, analyze whether the relevant feature qualifies as hosting/online platform and whether notice-and-action, statements of reasons, user terms and transparency duties apply.

## 4. Moderation is not identical to AI safety
A platform's handling of illegal content, its AI-generated content transparency, personal-data processing and copyrighted-material reuse can each involve separate regimes. Build distinct review tracks so a single “AI-safe” badge does not substitute for legal compliance.

## Exam
**Scenario:** A U.S. startup offers an EU-facing generative writing assistant, a public content marketplace and AI hiring scores. Create separate applicability grids for GDPR, AI Act, DSA, consumer rules and employment law. Explain which questions remain unresolved.

**Model answer:** Identify each feature's intended purpose and operator roles, audience and territorial reach. Compare Annex III hiring with lower-risk writing functionality, DSA hosting/platform conditions for the marketplace, GDPR personal-data flows, and each regime's independent duties. Distinguish enacted, currently applicable and future-dated obligations.
